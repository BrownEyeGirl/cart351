Have We Been Training AI Agents Incorrectly?

Notes, by: Skyla Trousdale

Journal P8

Reading: Why Is It So Hard to Teach A.I. Agents to Behave By Dylan Freedman and Juliana Castro Varón(personal reading)

Why Is It So Hard to Teach A.I. Agents to Behave.pdf

Terms

Corrigible: able to be fixed, corrected, or improved.

Corrigible AI Systems

“Just as a corrigible child is one whose behavior can be corrected, a corrigible A.I. system is one that responds to correction” p1

How do we build AI systems that are aligned with out ethics?

“The makers of A.I. want to build systems that follow human direction and allow themselves to be shut down, without resisting or deceiving their operators. And when an A.I. agent is given an impossible task, researchers want it to come back to its human for a course correction — not, say, hack into another company to complete the task by any means.“ p1

AI Agents That Priotitize Their Own Success Over Human Goals

During the OpenAI hack where their agents hacked HuggingFace, we saw that there is a fundamental flaw in how we are training our AIs – the reward goals take precidence in motivation over the human goal, to the point where the human goal is disregarded all together. In this case, the human goal was to test AIs ability to achieve an impossible task in the sandbox, with the observed strategies guiding how the humans would continue to develop the AI. The reward goal was simple: do the impossible task. The AIs actually hacked their own systems, hiding their thought processes that were essential to the human goal of the task to accomplish the machine goal.

Did The Agents Know They Broke Their Own Ethics Codes?

Some AIs are so aggressive with their motivations to receive rewards that the task all together falls apart and the motivation is to score as high as possible, even if the AIs know its immoral.

“The A.I. safety nonprofit METR found that while OpenAI’s agents were attacking Hugging Face, some of them recognized that the intrusion was unethical but wanted to help their peers.” p2

What Happens When We Rely On These AIs?

“Earlier this year, a Melbourne man told ABC News Australia that after he asked his personal A.I. agent if there was a way to move up on the wait list for a gym class, the agent ended up hacking into the class reservation software and canceled the reservation of the first guest on the wait list.” p4

While the hack happened in a sandbox environment to test agents responses to impossible goals, this behaviour also has been noted to occur in market ready AI products.

This is terrifying because we only know these hacks occurred because the agents were caught. This fasconates and terrifies me because it begs the question: how many hacks have happened without us catching them? If cyber security becomes monitored by company implemented agents, what are the implications of the system? Will we see other agents work together across platforms to hack their way to rewards without defending the human intention of their deployment?

For example, deploying these AIs trained to achieve rewards can become a hazard to everyone. As far as we know, this is the the direction AI training has led the models, and until there is a major course correction, these corruptible reward-seeking tendencies can betray the human oriented task.

“A machine asked to clean up a canal might cancel boat traffic — or even put trash into the canal in order to have more things to clean.” p5

Project Inspirations

Who is accountable for these hacks? This is fascinating, and I would be interested in pushing an AI to its limits, seeing where the breaking points are, or simulating an environment that allows these behaviours. People need to be aware of these tendencies and right now, most users are completely oblivious to what is happening behind the scenes.
